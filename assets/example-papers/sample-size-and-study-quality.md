# Is sample size the most important factor in the quality of a psychology study?

## Introduction

It is often said that a study with more participants is a better study. Students are taught to look at the "N" first, and reviewers often criticise papers for having small samples. This essay will argue that sample size is important, because it affects statistical power and the precision of estimates, but that it is not the most important factor in the quality of a psychology study. Measurement, design, the analysis plan and who the sample represents matter at least as much, and a very large sample can make some of these problems worse rather than better. The essay first explains why sample size matters, then discusses three other factors, and finally considers what this means for judging research.

## Why sample size matters

The main reason sample size matters is statistical power. Power is the probability that a study will detect an effect if the effect really exists. Cohen (1992) suggested that studies should aim for a power of 0.80, and showed that for a "medium" effect in a simple two-group comparison this needs roughly 64 participants per group. Many published psychology studies have far fewer than this. Button et al. (2013) estimated that the median statistical power of studies in neuroscience was only about 21%, which means that most real effects would be missed.

Low power does not only cause missed effects. It also means that the significant results which are published are more likely to be false positives, and that the effect sizes they report are likely to be exaggerated, because only the studies that happened to get a large estimate reach significance. Button et al. (2013) call this a "winner's curse". So a small study is not just less informative, it can actively mislead the literature when it is combined with publication bias.

Larger samples also give more precise estimates. A confidence interval gets narrower as the sample grows, so a researcher can say not only whether there is an effect but roughly how big it is. This is important in applied areas such as clinical psychology, where the size of an effect decides whether a treatment is worth using.

For these reasons, the replication crisis has often been described as a sample size problem. When the Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 studies from leading journals, only around 36% of the replications produced a significant result, and the average effect size was about half of the original. Small original samples are one explanation for this.

## Measurement

However, a large sample cannot rescue a poor measure. If a questionnaire does not measure the construct it claims to measure, then adding more participants only gives a more precise estimate of the wrong thing. For example, a study of "anxiety" that uses a single self-report item may have thousands of participants but still tell us little about anxiety as a clinician would understand it. Reliability matters too: unreliable measures add noise, which reduces power in a way that more participants can only partly compensate for.

Measurement is also linked to validity in a wider sense. Many psychology studies use convenience measures, such as reaction times in a computer task, as stand-ins for complex everyday behaviour. Whether the result generalises depends on whether the task captures the behaviour, and this is a question of theory and design, not of numbers.

## Design and analysis

The second factor is design. A well-designed experiment with random allocation to conditions can support a causal conclusion even with a modest sample, while a huge correlational survey cannot, however many people answer it. Confounding variables do not disappear when N increases. In fact, with a very large sample almost any correlation becomes statistically significant, so a large observational study can produce many "significant" findings that are trivial in size or caused by a third variable.

Flexible analysis is a related problem. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) showed that when researchers have freedom over which outcomes to report, when to stop collecting data and which covariates to include, the chance of a false positive can rise far above the nominal 5%. They demonstrated this by "showing" that listening to a particular song made participants younger. Notably, one of the flexible practices they describe is deciding sample size as you go, by checking the results and adding participants until the effect is significant. This shows that the process by which a sample size is chosen can matter as much as the final number. Pre-registration, where the hypotheses, sample size and analysis are fixed in advance, addresses this, and some journals now offer registered reports for this reason.

## Who is in the sample

The third factor is representativeness. Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010) pointed out that most psychology participants come from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic (WEIRD) societies, and often they are undergraduate students. They reviewed evidence that people from these societies are unusual on several measures that were assumed to be universal, such as some visual illusions and fairness in economic games. A sample of ten thousand American undergraduates is large but it is still a narrow sample, and conclusions about "human" psychology drawn from it may not hold elsewhere.

This means that a smaller study with a more diverse or more appropriate sample can sometimes be more valuable than a large one. For a study about older adults with dementia, for example, forty well-chosen participants may be worth more than four hundred students.

## Discussion

It could be argued that these other factors are all things that a careful researcher would get right anyway, and that sample size is the one thing that is most often neglected, so it deserves the most attention. There is some truth in this, as the low power estimates show. But the examples above suggest that the factors interact. Low power makes flexible analysis more dangerous, because small samples produce more variable results to choose between. Poor measurement lowers power. A narrow sample limits what a well-powered result means. So it is better to think of sample size as one part of a study's quality rather than as the most important part.

In practice, a reader judging a study should ask several questions: was the sample size justified in advance, for example with a power analysis; are the measures valid and reliable; does the design support the conclusion being drawn; was the analysis planned; and who were the participants. A study can do well on sample size and badly on all of the others.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, sample size is an important part of the quality of a psychology study because it determines power and precision, and the replication crisis has shown the costs of small samples. However, it is not the most important factor. Good measurement, an appropriate design, a pre-planned analysis and a suitable sample of participants are all needed, and a large sample cannot make up for weaknesses in these. The quality of a study depends on how all of these parts fit together.
